Plenty of books saved.
Still unsure what to read.

Narrow to Now

Our four-person team designed a Goodreads feature concept for readers who had books saved but still struggled to pick one. Narrow to Now lets readers add an optional saved reason, then uses mood, time and length to offer three books from their list with information to help compare them.

01 / Assignment 1 · Finding the right problem

The list kept growing.
The decision stayed difficult.

The problem we chose to solve

Readers had plenty of books saved, but still struggled to choose one. Their lists kept the titles without helping them remember why a book mattered or whether it suited what they wanted to read now.

P3 and P5 described this difficulty in our interviews. Their experience helped us see that keeping a recommendation and making a reading decision were separate needs.

“Goodreads became like a storage place instead of helping me choose.”P3 · Research interview

We focused on the moment a reader returned to their saved books. They needed to remember what had interested them, narrow the options to their current mood and available time, and decide which opinions to trust.

How we arrived at that problem

We hadn’t started with a saved-list feature in mind. We began by looking at how Goodreads supported discovery, evaluation and tracking, then followed readers’ experiences to understand where they needed help.

The Goodreads baseline

The original My Books page brings titles, authors, ratings and shelves into one table. In this view, the reader can see what is on their list, but still has to work out which book suits what they want to read now.

The survey changed the question

We looked through Goodreads’ search, recommendations, book pages and shelves. We also compared it with StoryGraph, Fable and Hardcover to see how other platforms approached the same tasks. This helped us prepare our research questions, but we still needed to hear from readers themselves.

The 41 survey responses changed how we approached the project. Many respondents found books outside Goodreads. So we stepped back and asked a broader question: how do people find something they want to read, and when does Goodreads become part of that decision?

Why we listened, then watched

The survey showed where readers found books. Six interviews helped explain how they judged them, and observing Goodreads use let us compare those habits with what happened on screen. Bringing these together helped us distinguish difficulties with finding a title from difficulties with choosing one.

Who we spoke to and how we approached the sessions

Participants read roughly two to 35 books a year: two had never used Goodreads, two had stopped using it, one used it rarely and one used it frequently. We recruited through people we could reach, so the findings describe this sample rather than all readers.

We asked, “Tell me about the last time you were looking for something to read.” Starting outside Goodreads let readers describe their own process before we introduced the platform.

We then watched them find a book on Goodreads, noting searches, pauses and moments of leaving the site. Asking what they were thinking, without suggesting actions, helped us compare their usual habits with what happened on screen.

What we did together

We compared notes across the survey, interviews and observations, looking for recurring patterns and differences. Bringing these perspectives together helped us agree on the problem to explore.

My part in Assignment 1

I helped plan the research, define who we wanted to speak to, prepare recruitment and screening materials, conduct interviews and write up findings. I also shared the survey and worked on Finding 2, about how readers use opinions they trust to judge books.

Readers moved between apps and websites

Readers moved between recommendations, reminders and reviews. Goodreads played a different role depending on where each person started.

Take a closer look at the reading journey

Three findings became three design needs

Finding 1 / Discovery happens elsewhere

Help readers after they find a recommendation.

“I still check Goodreads, I just don’t start there.”P5 · Research interview

Readers were already finding recommendations. We saw an opportunity to help after those recommendations reached a reading list.

Finding 2 / Evaluation depends on the source

Remember who recommended the book and why.

“If my friend says it’s good, that’s probably more convincing than a random rating.”P2 · Research interview

One participant checked Goodreads for the general response, then visited Reddit for another perspective. Readers weighed opinions differently depending on who shared them. This suggested that a friend’s recommendation was useful context to preserve alongside ratings and reviews.

Finding 3 / Saving a book doesn’t make choosing easier

More saved books can mean more indecision.

Keeping a recommendation and making a reading decision are different jobs. This finding shifted our focus towards helping readers narrow the books they already had, rather than adding more to the list.

From research to a design challenge

How might we help readers choose one book from their saved list when they want to read, without making saving harder?

This gave us three connected needs to design for: remember why a book mattered, narrow the saved options and judge the remaining choices. Those needs became the basis for comparing our ideas.

02 / Assignment 2 · Choosing a direction

Letting go of a good idea
for a more useful one.

With the problem defined, we explored twelve ways to help readers move from a saved recommendation to a reading decision.

What we did together

After the first round of ideas, we asked how readers would judge the picks and what someone with an empty list could do. A second round explored those gaps. We brought the sketches together before scoring them and testing two approaches with readers.

My part in Assignment 2

I sketched ideas G, H and I, helped test and score concepts, compared impact and effort, and designed the A2 Want to Read list with saved reasons.

In the A2 Want to Read list, I kept the saved reason beside each book. Labels such as “Mira’s rec” or “BookTok” carried the recommendation’s context into the later decision. This was how I brought my research finding about trusted opinions into the interface.

Smarter Search was easy to picture as a finished feature. But all six participants had found a known title without trouble in A1. That helped me set the idea aside and focus on choosing between saved books.

Three ideas became one reading decision

We scored each idea from one to three against four questions: does it help people pick from a long list, did we see the need in A1, would light readers use it, and could we build it for A3? We also compared the likely impact and effort to decide what we could reasonably take forward.

What we chose not to build

Recognising links and screenshots for Save from Anywhere was too much to build well within A3, and it wouldn’t resolve choosing from an existing list. Up Next overlapped with the shortlist. We also kept Ask My Friends for later to avoid expanding the core flow.

What we took from StoryGraph and TikTok

StoryGraph used mood, pace and length to filter books, while TikTok made saving quick. We wanted to keep that ease while helping readers remember why a book was on their list. None of our six interview participants mentioned using StoryGraph, so this comparison helped us explore possibilities rather than explain their habits.

Testing two ways to reduce a list

Five readers from our earlier interviews tried two ideas. First they answered questions to get three picks with A. Then they chose between pairs of books with E. Three preferred A, one preferred E and one saw value in both. A worked well when someone knew what mood they wanted. E helped when they found that harder to describe. Everyone tried A first, so the order may have affected their preference.

Feedback that changed the prototype

Readers should be able to skip a preference they don’t have.

We added “Any” to every question and a Change answers link on the picks page. Someone could ask for a short book without having to choose a mood. We kept pairwise comparison as a possible idea for browsing later, while focusing this version on three questions and three picks.

We took these decisions into A3, where the next question was whether the connected experience would work in use.

03 / Assignment 3 · Saving and refinement

Save first.
Ask for context after.

The feedback made us look at the whole experience

The A2 feedback challenged us to explain our scope, support books without reasons and connect saving with choosing. We needed to show how the complete experience helped someone make a decision.

What we did together

We connected our screens into one prototype, checking that saved reasons and book states stayed consistent between each person’s designs. We then tested the journey from saving a recommendation to choosing a book.

My part in Assignment 3

I designed the book page, optional reason selection, skip path and saved confirmation. I also helped prepare the saving walkthrough and findings. My usability-testing work is described in the evaluation below.

In A2, the reader had to get through the reason question before saving was complete. That added a step to something that should feel quick.

I changed the order so the first tap saves the book to Want to Read. After that, the reader can add a reason or skip it. They can remember a friend’s recommendation or a BookTok discovery without having to explain every book they save.

02 · Save / Actual A3 prototype

Saving flow · Michelle. The book is already saved. Readers can add a reason or skip it.

Give readers a clear next step

The same saved reason appears on the list, shortlist and book page. Questions appear one at a time, with earlier answers visible. Every pick leads to a clear next action:

Start reading moves the book to Currently Reading. Not now keeps it saved but hides it from picks for 30 days. Remove takes it off Want to Read, and Undo lets readers reverse a change.

Keep the experience familiar

We kept the book typography, warm colours and shelves familiar to Goodreads users. Buttons, tags, fit summaries and confirmation messages followed the same patterns across the prototype. Selected answers show a tick as well as a filled background. We also designed for 44-pixel tap targets.

05 / Assignment 3 · Evaluation

Quicker choices.
Still room for clarity.

We compared Narrow to Now with a reconstructed Goodreads baseline, then checked the saving and recovery flows.

What we asked readers to do

Five readers aged 20–23 joined the Zoom study. They had different levels of familiarity with Goodreads and used a desktop Figma prototype containing 43 saved books. We asked them to think aloud during four tasks, then discussed what felt unclear:

  1. Choose on the baseline. Find a cosy, short book for a free weekend.
  2. Choose with Narrow to Now. Repeat the same scenario using the new flow.
  3. Save a recommendation. Save a book recommended by a friend; observe whether a reason is added.
  4. Recover from unhelpful picks. Decide what to do when none of the three feels right.

I facilitated P3’s session, took notes for P4, wrote up P3’s findings and brought the anonymised notes together.

06 / Looking back

What I learned
and would do differently.

Letting go of an idea I could already picture

Setting aside Smarter Search was a useful lesson for me. I could imagine how the interface would look, but that didn’t make it the right answer to our research. Comparing it with the readers’ actual difficulty helped me choose where to spend my effort. I want to carry that habit into future projects: ask what an idea helps someone do before focusing on how it could look.

The trade-off: useful context without extra work

For the saving flow, I had to balance two needs. A reason could help someone choose later, but recording it shouldn’t be a condition for saving a book. I made reasons optional, accepting that some books would have no saved context. That meant the team’s shortlist also needed to work from mood, pace and length tags. I learned to think about what an optional step means for the rest of the experience, not just the screen I was designing.

A saved book still needs to feel saved

The saving task showed me that getting the order of actions right wasn’t enough. I had separated saving from adding a reason, yet some readers were still unsure what skipping would do. The challenge was making the completed action understandable. I learned to pay more attention to confirmation messages and the questions a reader might still have after clicking.

What I would improve

If I revisited this design, I would make the saved confirmation more noticeable before showing the optional reason question. With the team, I would also revisit the results controls, using “Show 3 more picks” and “Change my preferences” to make the difference between the actions clearer.

In a similar project, I would consider skipping, having no preference and rejecting every suggestion alongside the main flow from the start. Those situations helped reveal assumptions that were easy to miss when focusing on a smooth path through the screens.
